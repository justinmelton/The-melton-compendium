A compendium of genealogical research, compiled by Justin Melton — the Lacie, Moore, Shrum, Freeman, Marler, Carter & allied lines of Oklahoma and Arkansas

Welcome

New here? The easiest way in is the Family Tree. Tap the green button, find a name you know, and tap it — that person's page will open. Every page has a big button at the top to bring you back.

This site is the family's own archive: every person researched, every record found, every photograph and story — gathered person by person, checked against documents, and kept here so nothing is lost and anyone in the family can reach it from anywhere.

It grows as the research grows. Each completed investigation adds a page for that person and adds their records to the Sources hub, where every document carries a permanent number. Our printed family books cite those numbers, so a book never goes out of date — the full record is always one scan away.

How to use this site

Start at the Family Tree and click any name to open that person's page, or browse everyone under People. Each page tells one person's life as a story, in the order it happened — and everything in it is proven: each fact is backed by a record in the archive, and the records behind each story are named at the foot of the page. If a fact isn't proven yet, it isn't on this site. It stays in the research files until a record settles it.

Our Method

Two identifiers, or it isn't proven

A fact is published here only when two independent identifiers agree — a name plus a date, a place, a spouse, a household. That is the bar for everything on this website.

What you won't find here

Guesses. When records disagree, or a claim rests on one source alone, or a match is only a maybe, it does not go on this website. It stays in the research files — flagged, with the gap named — until a confirming record settles it one way or the other. We would rather leave a blank than print a maybe.

Everything is kept locally

Every find is saved as a file in the family archive, not just a web link, so the evidence survives even if a website changes or disappears.

Privacy

Living people's private details do not appear on this site. Certain documents — a veteran's DD214 among them — are cited as "on file" and never displayed, because they carry information that isn't ours to publish.

About the Author

The keepers before me

I come from a family that kept its own history. My grandma, Lutricia, can tell you about kin she never met like they were just in the next room — who they married, where they lived, where they lie. My mom, Denise, has done genealogy research for years: binders of it, records she's chased down, a whole compiled history of one of our lines that she keeps and works from. I grew up around their kitchen-table knowledge without ever calling it research. It was just how the family remembered itself.

The fire

When I was in my early twenties, the house my great-grandpa Freeman built with his own bare hands burned to the ground. A great deal of the family's photographs and records were in that house, and they went with it. That's the hole in the middle of our family history — pictures that exist now only in somebody's memory of them, papers nobody can pull out of a drawer anymore. Whatever copies survive sit scattered with the other elders' branches of the family, and part of this work is simply asking around before those are gone too.

Why I do this

So I started doing what my mom and grandma had always done — asking, collecting, writing it down — and then I took it a step further: checking the stories against the records. Courthouse marriage books. Census pages. Old newspapers. The results surprised me. Some stories hold up completely. Some bend. Some nobody can prove at all, and I say so when that's the case. Everything in this archive is held to one standard: two records agreeing, or it's marked probable, with the gap left showing. I'd rather hand my family an honest gap than a pretty story that isn't true.

There's a great-uncle of mine whose voice I've never heard. A recording of him sits in a state archive, and getting it is on my list — because after the fire, I know exactly what it means to almost lose a piece of somebody forever.

And I'm doing it now, on purpose, while my mom and grandma are still here to ask.

What I hope it becomes

A shelf of books for the family, this website standing behind them with every source a scan away. Something built to still be here in fifty years, so that when my grandkids ask who these people were, the answers are waiting for them: names with faces, stories with records behind them, and nothing left as just a date on a stone.

— Justin Melton
